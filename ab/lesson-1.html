Learning Targets & Vocabulary

I can…

  • Interpret \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) from graphs and tables.
  • Use left/right behavior to decide if a limit exists.
  • Evaluate simple limits by substitution or simplification.
  • Explain the difference between \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) and \(f(a)\).
Success criteria: you can explain why a limit exists/doesn’t, not only compute it.

The Big Idea (Brief, Powerful)

Informal definition: \[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x)=L \] means: if \(x\) gets close to \(a\) (but not necessarily equal to \(a\)), then \(f(x)\) gets close to \(L\).

Two rules of the universe

  • Limits care about what happens near \(a\), not necessarily at \(a\).
  • A two-sided limit exists only if left limit = right limit.

Tap: Formal definition (optional)

\[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x)=L \;\;\text{means: for every }\varepsilon>0\text{ there exists }\delta>0 \] such that if \(0<|x-a|<\delta\), then \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\).
You do not need to master this today — but we’ll play with it later in the sandbox.

Definition Sandbox (Optional, but Awesome): ε–δ

We’ll use \(f(x)=x^2\), \(a=2\), \(L=4\). Your mission: choose \(\delta\) so that \(0<|x-2|<\delta\) forces \(|x^2-4|<\varepsilon\).
ε–δ Visual
a: 2 L: 4 ε: δ:

What to notice

  • ε controls the vertical “target band” around \(L\).
  • δ controls the horizontal window around \(a\).
  • We need the curve inside the δ-window to stay inside the ε-band.
In AB, you mostly use the informal definition + tools. This sandbox makes the formal idea feel real.
